5G NETWORKS

Advanced Examination Questions and Answers 
Question 1: 
Explain how network slicing improves Quality of Service (QoS) in 5G networks. 
Answer: 
Network slicing improves QoS by allocating dedicated virtual network resources to specific 
services. This ensures predictable latency, bandwidth, and reliability, preventing performance degradation caused by traffic congestion from other services. 
Question 2: 
Discuss the role of edge computing in achieving ultra-low latency in 5G applications. 
Answer: 
Edge computing reduces latency by processing data near the end user instead of relying on 
centralized cloud servers. This minimizes transmission delays and supports real-time applications such as autonomous vehicles and remote surgery. 
Question 3: 
Compare Standalone (SA) and Non-Standalone (NSA) 5G architectures. 
Answer: 
NSA architecture relies on existing 4G infrastructure for control signaling, allowing faster deployment but limiting advanced features. SA architecture uses a full 5G core, enabling network slicing, ultra-low latency, and full 5G capabilities. 
Question 4: 
Why is mmWave deployment challenging despite its high data rates? 
Answer: 
mmWave signals have limited propagation range and are easily blocked by buildings, 
vegetation, and weather conditions. This necessitates dense base station deployment, increasing infrastructure costs. 
Question 5: 
Explain how 5G acts as a foundation for 6G development. 
Answer: 
5G introduces cloud-native cores, Al-assisted network management, and advanced spectrum 
utilization. These technologies form the baseline upon which 6G will build, enabling intelligent, 
autonomous, and globally connected networks. 

DEEP LEARNING

Question 1: Deep Learning Training and Optimization
(a) Describe the main stages involved in training a deep learning model.
(b) Explain the role of activation functions in deep neural networks.
(c) State and briefly explain two optimization techniques used to improve
deep learning training.
Answer
(a)
Training a deep learning model involves three main stages: forward
propagation, loss computation, and backpropagation. In forward
propagation, input data passes through the network to generate predictions.
The loss function then measures the difference between predicted and actual
outputs. Backpropagation computes gradients and updates model weights
iteratively using optimization algorithms.
(b)
Activation functions introduce non-linearity into neural networks, allowing
them to learn complex patterns. Without activation functions, deep networks
would behave like linear models regardless of depth.
(c)
Two optimization techniques are:
 Regularization, such as dropout or L1/L2 regularization, which helps
prevent overfitting.
 Batch normalization, which stabilizes learning by normalizing
activations and allows faster convergence.
Question 2: Deep Learning Architectures and Evaluation
(a) Compare Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) and Convolutional Neural
Networks (CNNs) in terms of structure and data suitability.
(b) Explain why Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) may struggle with
long-term dependencies.
(c) Describe two evaluation metrics used to assess deep learning model
performance.

Answer
(a)
ANNs are fully connected networks suitable for structured or tabular data,
while CNNs use convolutional filters and shared weights, making them
more efficient and effective for spatial data such as images.
(b)
RNNs may struggle with long-term dependencies due to the vanishing
gradient problem, where gradients become too small during
backpropagation, preventing the network from learning long-range
relationships.
(c)
Two evaluation metrics are:
 Accuracy, which measures the proportion of correctly classified
instances.
 F1-score, which balances precision and recall to provide a more
reliable measure of model performance.

GAME DESIGN AND DEV

QUESTION ONE:
Explain the concept of Game Design and discuss its key components.
ANSWER:

Game design is the art and science of creating the structure and experience of a game by defining
rules, mechanics, objectives, player interactions, and progression systems. It focuses on how
players think, feel, and behave while playing. Game design determines how a game works and
how challenges and rewards are structured.

The key components of game design include:
1. Mechanics: These are the rules and systems that govern gameplay, such as jumping,
shooting, or scoring.
2. Dynamics: These describe how mechanics interact with player behavior during
gameplay.
3. Aesthetics: These refer to the emotional experiences, visuals, and overall feel of the
game.
4. Gameplay: This involves the actions, challenges, and interactions players experience in
the game.
Together, these elements shape the player’s experience and determine the success of a game.

QUESTION TWO:
Differentiate between Real-time Animation and Pre-rendered Animation in
game development.
Real-time animation is animation that is generated instantly during gameplay and changes
according to player actions. For example, a character running or fighting in games like GTA or
FIFA is real-time animation.
Pre-rendered animation, on the other hand, is created in advance and played as a fixed video or
cutscene in a game. It does not change based on player input. Examples include story cutscenes
in games like God of War or Uncharted.
In summary, real-time animation is interactive and dynamic, while pre-rendered animation is
fixed and cinematic.

QUESTION 1 SOLUTIONS
Part (a): Define Bioinformatics (4 marks)
Bioinformatics is the intersection of biology, computer science, math, and statistics used to
analyze biological data at the molecular level. It emerged because scientists needed
computational tools to handle the massive amounts of DNA, RNA, and protein data from modern
sequencing technologies, data too vast to analyze manually.
Part (b): Three Classifications of Bioinformatics

1. Sequence Bioinformatics - Analyzes DNA/RNA/protein sequences (linear strings of
A,T,G,C or amino acids). Uses tools like BLAST to compare sequences, find genes, and
identify patterns.
2. Structural Bioinformatics - Predicts and analyzes 3D shapes of proteins and nucleic
acids. Uses databases like PDB and tools like AlphaFold for structure prediction and drug
design.
3. Functional Bioinformatics - Determines what genes and proteins actually do in cells.
Includes gene expression analysis, protein interactions, and metabolic pathways.
Part (c): Black Box Problem
AI models in bioinformatics make accurate predictions (e.g., "this mutation causes disease") but
cannot explain why or how. This lack of transparency creates problems in clinical settings where
doctors need to understand reasoning, not just accept predictions. It also hinders scientific
understanding.
Part (d): Forensic Applications
Application 1 - Paternity Testing: Analyzes DNA markers (STRs) to determine biological
relationships with >99.9% certainty for legal cases like custody disputes.
Application 2 - Disaster Victim Identification: Matches DNA from remains to missing persons
databases or family members. Used after tsunamis, plane crashes, etc., to identify bodies when
traditional methods fail.
QUESTION 2
Part (a): Data Heterogeneity (4 marks)
Biological data exists in many incompatible formats (FASTQ, BAM, VCF, PDB, etc.) across
disconnected databases worldwide. This fragmentation makes combining data difficult
researchers spend significant time converting formats and harmonizing different standards before
analysis can begin.
Part (b): Agricultural Applications (8 marks)
Application 1 - Climate-Resilient Crops: Genomic analysis identifies drought/flood/heat
resistance genes by comparing plant genomes. Example: Discovered SUB1A gene allowing rice
to survive 2-week floods, now grown across Asia protecting millions.

Application 2 - Food Safety: Sequences pathogen DNA from outbreaks to trace contamination
sources (specific farms/facilities) within days using phylogenetic analysis. Enables targeted
recalls protecting consumers.
Part (c): Interdisciplinary Nature
Biology - Provides knowledge of genes, proteins, cells; essential for understanding what data
means biologically.
Computer Science - Supplies programming, algorithms, databases, software engineering; enables
data processing and tool development.
Mathematics/Statistics - Offers statistical methods, probability, modeling; determines if patterns
are significant or random chance.
Each discipline is essential no single field alone can do bioinformatics effectively.

 Answer: Stereoscopy is based on the principle of Binocular Disparity, where

the brain into perceiving depth in a digital environment. Lenses are critical
because they allow the user&#39;s eyes to focus on a screen that is physically only
inches away, making the virtual world appear at a natural distance.
1b) Compare Fresnel Lenses with modern &quot;Pancake Lenses&quot; in terms of their

 Answer: Fresnel Lenses are legacy components that use concentric rings to
focus light but are bulky and prone to glare known as &quot;god rays&quot;. In contrast,
modern Pancake Lenses fold the optical path using polarization, which allows
for headsets that are significantly thinner, lighter, and offer superior edge-to-
edge clarity.

 Answer: The brain relies on the eyes, the inner ear, and the body&#39;s muscles and
joints to understand motion. Motion sickness occurs in VR when a natural
alignment is broken for example, if a user’s eyes see rapid virtual movement
while their inner ear and body remain stationary, the resulting sensory
mismatch creates confusion in the brain.

understanding the physical geometry of the room. Mixed Reality (MR) is a
hybrid blend where digital and physical objects coexist and interact in real-time
for example, a virtual ball can bounce off a real table or a virtual screen can be

COMPUTER VISION

Question 1: Architecture Deep-Dive
Distinguish between CNNs and Vision Transformers (ViTs) based on their "Inductive Bias" and "Receptive Fields" 
Answer
Inductive Bias: CNNs have a "strong" bias because they are hard-wired to understand locality and translation invariance. ViTs have a "weak" bias, meaning they don't make assumptions about the data and must learn all spatial relationships from scratch.

Receptive Field: CNNs process images locally (focusing on small neighborhoods of pixels), whereas ViTs have a global receptive field from the start, allowing them to see the entire image at once via self-attention.



Question 2: Pipeline Mechanics & Bottlenecks
Outline the six steps of the standard Computer Vision pipeline and explain briefly state what is done in each step
Answer
The 6-Step Pipeline:
Image Acquisition: Capturing data via sensors/cameras.
Preprocessing: Cleaning data (noise reduction, resizing).
Feature Detection: Identifying edges, corners, or blobs.
Feature Description: Creating mathematical maps of those features.
Pattern Recognition: Classifying the objects using ML or Deep Learning.
Post-Processing: Refining the output for final decision-making.

 How it works: The model acts like a student studying a past exam. It looks at the

Question 2: In the document, machine learning model was compared to a student

WIRELESS SENSOR NETWORKS

1. Define Wireless Sensor Networks (WSNs) and state their major applications.
Answer:
Wireless Sensor Networks (WSNs) are networks of small sensor nodes used to
monitor environmental or physical conditions such as temperature, humidity, and
pressure, and transmit the collected data wirelessly to a base station. They enable
automated and real-time monitoring with little human involvement.
WSNs are used in environmental monitoring, healthcare, agriculture, military
surveillance, industrial automation, and Internet of Things (IoT) applications.
2. State the main components of a sensor node in a Wireless Sensor Network
and their functions.
Answer:
A sensor node consists of a sensing unit that detects physical parameters, a
processing unit that processes and controls data, a communication unit that
transmits data wirelessly, and a power unit that supplies energy to the node. Each
component works together to enable data collection and transmission in the network.

NATURAL LANGUAGE PROCESSING

1. Define Natural Language Processing (NLP) and explain its role in bridging the gap
between computational techniques and linguistic knowledge.
2. List and explain the four primary steps involved in cleaning raw text for analysis.

ANSWERS
1. Natural Language Processing (NLP)
Natural Language Processing (NLP) is the intersection of computer science,
artificial intelligence, and linguistics. At its core, it is the technology used to help
computers "understand," interpret, and generate human language in a way that is
both meaningful and useful.
Bridging the Gap: Computation vs. Linguistics
The primary challenge in NLP is that human language is messy, ambiguous, and
context-dependent, while computers require structured, precise input. NLP acts
as the bridge by merging two distinct worlds:
i. Linguistic Knowledge: This provides the "rules of the game." It involves
understanding Syntax (grammar and sentence structure), Semantics (the
meaning of words), and Pragmatics (how context affects meaning). Without
linguistics, a computer might treat "The record was broken" and "Please record
the meeting" as identical because the words look the same.
ii. Computational Techniques: This is the "engine." It involves using algorithms,
statistical models, and deep learning architectures (like Transformers) to process
massive amounts of text. Computers use these techniques to represent words as
mathematical vectors in a multi-dimensional space.
By combining these, NLP transforms unstructured "human" data into a structured
format that a machine can perform operations on—allowing for tasks like
sentiment analysis, translation, and real-time conversation.
2. The 4 Primary Steps of Text Cleaning
Before a machine can analyze text, the data must be "cleaned" to remove noise
and standardize the format. This process is often called Preprocessing.
i. Tokenization
This is the process of breaking down a large body of text into smaller units called
tokens. Usually, these tokens are individual words, but they can also be characters or
sub-words.

 Example: "I love NLP!" becomes ["I", "love", "NLP", "!"].
 Why: It is the foundational step that allows the computer to count and track
individual linguistic units.
ii. Lowercasing
Text is converted entirely to lowercase to ensure uniformity.
 Example: "Apple," "APPLE," and "apple" are all reduced to apple.
 Why: To a computer, "Apple" and "apple" have different ASCII values and would
be treated as different entities. Lowercasing reduces the "vocabulary" size and helps the
model realize they are the same concept.
iii. Stop Word Removal
"Stop words" are common words that carry very little unique information, such as "the,"
"is," "at," "which," and "on."
 Example: "The cat is on the mat" becomes ["cat", "mat"].
 Why: These words appear so frequently that they can drown out the "signal" of
the more important keywords. Removing them helps the model focus on the words that
actually define the meaning of the sentence.
iv. Lemmatization (or Stemming)
This step involves reducing words to their root or base form.
 Stemming is a "crude" method that chops off the ends of words (e.g., "running"
becomes "run").
 Lemmatization is a more sophisticated method that uses a dictionary to find the
actual root (the "lemma").
 Example: "Better" $\rightarrow$ "Good"; "Running" $\rightarrow$ "Run"; "Mice"
$\rightarrow$ "Mouse".
 Why: This groups different forms of the same word together so the machine
understands they represent the same underlying idea.

Answers

Question 1
(a) Cybersecurity: This is the practice of protecting systems, networks, and programs from
digital attacks. It is a multifaceted discipline combining people, processes, and technology to
defend against threats aimed at accessing, changing, or destroying sensitive information.
The CIA Triad: This represents three fundamental security objectives:
- Confidentiality: Ensures information is accessible only to authorized users, preventing
unauthorized access through encryption and passwords.
- Integrity: Ensures data remains accurate, complete, and unaltered unless modified by
authorized users.
- Availability: Ensures systems, networks, and data are accessible to authorized users
whenever needed.

(b) Differences between Threat, Attack, and Risk

- Cyber Threat: A potential danger or possibility that could exploit a weakness in a system
to cause harm. It exists even if no action has occurred yet (e.g., hackers targeting banks).
- Cyber Attack: The actual action taken by an attacker to harm, disrupt, or gain
unauthorized access to a system (e.g., launching ransomware).
- Cyber Risk: The likelihood and impact of a cyber threat turning into a successful attack.
It is used for decision-making and budgeting.

(c) Categories of Threat Actors
- Cybercriminals: Individuals or organized groups who commit illegal cyber activities
primarily for financial gain.
- Nation-State Actors: Governments or state-supported teams that launch attacks for
political, military, or intelligence reasons, often engaging in espionage or cyberwarfare.
- Hacktivists: Hackers driven by political, social, or ideological motives rather than money,
attacking systems to protest or expose perceived wrongdoing

Question 2
(a) Social Engineering and Techniques
Concept: Social engineering involves manipulating people into sharing information they
shouldn&#39;t share, downloading malicious software, or making mistakes that compromise security.
It relies on psychological manipulation (exploiting trust, fear, or urgency) rather than breaking
technical defenses.
Techniques:
- Phishing: Deceiving victims via email (or SMS/voice) into revealing sensitive
information like passwords or credit card numbers by impersonating reputable sources.
- Tailgating: A physical or digital attack where an unauthorized person follows an
authorized person into a prohibited area (e.g., sneaking into a building behind someone or
using an employee&#39;s unattended device).

(b) Network vs. Endpoint Security:

- Network Security: Focuses on securing the network itself (both internal and external)
from unauthorized access using tools like firewalls and VPNs.
- Endpoint Security: Focuses on protecting individual devices (laptops, smartphones,
servers) that connect to the network, often using antivirus software and device
encryption.

IDS vs. IPS:
- Intrusion Detection System (IDS): Monitors network activities for suspicious behavior
and alerts administrators but does not block traffic automatically.
- Intrusion Prevention System (IPS): Detects malicious activity and takes action to prevent
it, such as blocking traffic or terminating suspicious connections in real-time.

(c) AI&#39;s Role and Cybersecurity Challenges
Role of AI: AI processes large amounts of data to identify patterns indicating malicious activity.
It helps in Threat Detection by monitoring user behavior and Malware Classification by
analyzing the behavior of programs to detect unknown or new threats.
Challenges:
- Lack of Awareness: Many users are unaware of common threats like phishing, leading to
careless actions like using weak passwords.
- Insider Threats: Threats coming from employees or partners with legitimate access are
difficult to detect because they operate within normal access privileges

QUESTION 1
1a. Define Blockchain Technology and explain its relationship with
Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT).
Blockchain technology is a decentralized digital ledger that records transactions

1

across multiple computers (nodes) in a way that prevents alteration or tampering.
Information is grouped into blocks, and each block is linked to the previous
block, forming a chain that is secure, transparent, and immutable.
Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT) is the broader concept behind
blockchain. DLT refers to a digital system where records are stored across
multiple computers instead of one central server. Each node has an identical
copy of the ledger, and updates are synchronized across the network. This makes
the system decentralized, transparent, tamper-resistant, and highly secure.
Relationship between Blockchain and DLT:
Blockchain is described in the document as a highly specialized form of DLT.
While DLTs are generally shared encrypted databases, blockchain is distinguished
by its specific structure: data is stored in sequentially chained blocks,
producing an irreversible and incorruptible record. Access and authentication
are managed using cryptographic keys and signatures, which makes blockchain
a secure way to implement DLT in real-world systems.

1b. Discuss three (3) core components that ensure the functionality and
security of blockchain systems.
From the document, blockchain functionality and security depend on
interconnected components that work together to create trusted, decentralized
systems.
(i) Cryptography
Cryptography is presented as the “heart” of blockchain security. It uses
mathematical algorithms such as digital signatures and hash functions to
protect the system against unauthorized access and tampering. It ensures:
 secure flow of information,
 transaction confidentiality,
 authentication and integrity of data.
In simple terms, cryptography ensures that transactions are legitimate and that the
stored records cannot be altered without detection.
(ii) Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT) / Distributed Structure
The distributed ledger ensures that data is not stored in one place. Instead, it is
shared across many nodes in a peer-to-peer network, giving participants access to
a single version of truth. This creates:
 transparency (because records are shared),
 decentralization (because there is no single controller),
 resilience (because one failure does not collapse the entire system).
(iii) Consensus Mechanisms
Consensus mechanisms are the procedural rules that allow nodes to agree on the
validity of transactions and the correct ordering of blocks. Since no central
authority exists, the network must collectively validate what is true. Consensus
therefore:
 ensures only valid transactions are added,

1
 prevents manipulation,
 maintains agreement across the distributed network.
Note: The paper also includes smart contracts as another core component—self-
executing programs stored on the blockchain that enforce agreements
automatically when conditions are met. They reduce fraud and eliminate
middlemen.

1c. Explain three (3) key features of blockchain technology and how each
contributes to trust and transparency in digital systems.
The document highlights multiple features; here are three key ones clearly linked
to trust and transparency:
(i) Immutability
Immutability means that once data is recorded and sealed into a block, it cannot
be altered, deleted, or removed retroactively unless the majority of the network
approves. This builds trust because records become reliable and permanent,
supporting audit trails and accountability.
(ii) Decentralization
Decentralization means the network is not controlled by a single authority.
Instead, data is stored and managed across multiple nodes. This improves trust
because:
 there is no single point of control,
 there is no single point of failure,
 users do not need to rely on one organization to “tell the truth.”
(iii) Transparency
In public permissionless blockchains, transactions are visible publicly, and
participants share one consistent record. This transparency strengthens trust
because people can verify activities independently rather than depending on
hidden records controlled by a middleman.

2a. Describe the four major blockchain network architectures: Public,
Private, Consortium, Hybrid.
The document explains that blockchain architecture has different network
models, each with trade-offs in access, governance, privacy, and scalability.
(i) Public Blockchains (Permissionless)
 Open to anyone globally: anyone can read, write, and validate transactions.
 Governance is fully decentralized (no central authority).
 Offers maximum transparency but tends to be slower and less scalable due to
complex consensus required among many participants.
Examples given include Bitcoin and Ethereum.
(ii) Private Blockchains (Permissioned)
 Participation is restricted; users require authorization.

1

 Governance is centralized under one entity or a small group.
 Advantages: higher throughput, more privacy, cheaper and less energy-
intensive (lighter consensus).
 Trade-off: reduced transparency and greater centralized control.
(iii) Consortium (Federated) Blockchains
 Controlled by multiple organizations (shared authority).
 Governance is distributed among known participants.
 More efficient and scalable than public chains because fewer nodes are needed
for consensus.
 Risk: if a member node is compromised, the entire system can be affected.
Examples mentioned include consortium-style systems like Corda or
Quorum (as referenced in the paper’s description).
(iv) Hybrid Blockchains
 Combine public and private strengths.
 A private blockchain handles internal confidential operations, while selected
data is anchored/shared publicly for verification and trust.
 Offers flexibility: private privacy + public auditability.
 Useful for sectors needing both confidentiality and public verification (e.g.,
healthcare, supply chain, government services).

2b. Explain blockchain governance, distinguishing between on-chain
governance, off-chain governance, and hybrid governance.
Blockchain governance refers to the structures and decision-making processes
that determine how a blockchain network is managed, updated, and kept secure.
Because blockchains often have no central authority, governance ensures the
system can evolve, resolve conflicts, and remain sustainable.
(i) On-chain governance
 Rules and decision processes are built into the blockchain protocol.
 Participants (token holders, validators, miners) vote on proposals using
blockchain mechanisms.
 Decision-making becomes transparent and automated, often via smart
contracts.
(ii) Off-chain governance
 Decisions are made outside the blockchain system through:
discussions, community forums, developer meetings, or foundation-led
processes.
 These decisions still influence upgrades because developers implement them
in future updates.
(iii) Hybrid governance
Although the document focuses on on-chain and off-chain, its discussion of
mixed structures (especially hybrid models) supports a clear explanation of
hybrid governance:
Hybrid governance is a blend where some decisions are encoded and voted on

1

via on-chain procedures, while other decisions occur through off-chain
community and organizational processes. This approach attempts to balance
transparency and automation with real-world coordination among stakeholders.

2c. Discuss three (3) major advantages of blockchain technology and explain
how they improve efficiency and security in digital systems.
From the document’s advantages section, three major advantages are:
(i) Transparency and Traceability
Blockchain provides a shared, verifiable record (“one version of truth”). This
improves efficiency by supporting easier auditing and verification, and it
improves security by reducing fraud and hidden manipulation.
(ii) Security and Data Integrity
Because blockchain uses cryptographic hashing and consensus validation,

in the system’s records.
(iii) Automation through Smart Contracts
Smart contracts execute agreements automatically when conditions are met. This

opportunities.

2d. Identify and explain three (3) limitations of blockchain technology that
may hinder its widespread adoption.
The document lists several limitations; three major ones are:
(i) Scalability Constraints
Public blockchains often struggle with low throughput and slow transaction

(ii) Energy Consumption
Some consensus methods—especially Proof-of-Work—require large
computational power, leading to high energy consumption and environmental
sustainability concerns. This can discourage adoption by governments and
enterprises.
(iii) Regulatory and Legal Uncertainty
The paper notes that governments are still developing regulations for
cryptocurrencies, digital identities, tokenized assets, and decentralized
applications. This uncertainty affects organizational adoption and investor
confidence because stakeholders may fear future restrictions or legal risks.
